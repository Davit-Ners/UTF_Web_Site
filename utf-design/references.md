# Visual References — Until They Fall Simple Website

These references are not templates to reproduce.
Each one is included for one or two specific design lessons.

The objective is to extract a visual principle, then reinterpret it through Until They Fall's own assets, photography, Gerard, Tower Seven and the band's existing identity.

Do not mix every idea from every reference into the same screen.

---

## REF-01 — Arctic Monkeys Editorial Website

**File:** `references/REF-01-arctic-monkeys-editorial.jpg`

### What we like

- Strong editorial rhythm rather than repeated website sections.
- Large areas of negative space.
- Photography exists directly in the composition instead of inside generic cards.
- Different sections use different spatial relationships.
- Black background does not require decorative gradients or UI chrome.
- Small information can coexist with very large visual moments.

### What we do NOT want to copy

- The exact typography.
- The extreme amount of emptiness everywhere.
- The exact monochrome identity.
- The historic / retro feeling as a brand direction for UTF.

### Useful for

- Homepage rhythm.
- About section.
- Gallery teaser.
- General spacing and asymmetry.

### Extracted rule

> Treat the homepage like an editorial composition, not a vertical stack of components.

## REF-02 — Tour Dates Poster

**File:** `references/REF-02-tour-dates-poster.jpg`

### What we like

- Concert information is treated as part of a poster rather than a database table.
- Live photography remains the emotional anchor.
- Date, venue and city stay highly scannable despite the expressive layout.
- Grain and print texture make the information feel physical.
- Red is used selectively to create hierarchy.

### What we do NOT want to copy

- The exact red / black identity.
- The exact distressed typeface.
- Too much visual noise around practical ticket information.
- A literal poster pasted into the website.

### Useful for

- Live section.
- Concerts page.
- Multiple-date state.
- Promotional show artwork.

### Extracted rule

> Tour dates should feel like a piece of band artwork while remaining instantly readable.

## REF-03 — ZEP USA Tour Poster

**File:** `references/REF-03-zep-tour-typography.jpg`

### What we like

- Oversized typography becomes part of the architecture of the composition.
- The main word is not merely a heading; it creates the visual structure.
- The photograph and type interact rather than living in separate boxes.
- Small tour information is clearly subordinate to the primary visual idea.
- Strong use of scale contrast.

### What we do NOT want to copy

- The literal `USA` composition.
- The exact typeface.
- The beige / retro colour treatment.
- Huge typography when it harms mobile readability.

### Useful for

- Live section.
- Section titles.
- Gallery transitions.
- Release names.

### Extracted rule

> Major words and dates can function as graphic shapes, not only as labels.

## REF-04 — The Weeknd Editorial Layout

**File:** `references/REF-04-weeknd-editorial.jpg`

### What we like

- Portrait photography and typography deliberately overlap.
- Black-and-white imagery gives a strong editorial / magazine quality.
- Different content blocks use different proportions without becoming chaotic.
- Outlined typography can create secondary hierarchy without adding another colour.
- Biography / track information is integrated into the visual composition.

### What we do NOT want to copy

- The exact artist branding.
- Russian editorial text styling.
- Every outlined type treatment.
- A clean fashion-editorial feeling that becomes too polished for UTF.

### Useful for

- About section.
- Gallery.
- Band photography.
- Release detail layouts.

### Extracted rule

> Let text and photography share the same visual space instead of isolating them in independent blocks.

## REF-05 — Lindemann Red Identity

**File:** `references/REF-05-lindemann-red-identity.jpg`

### What we like

- Extremely coherent identity across the whole page.
- One strong colour dominates instead of multiple decorative accents.
- Photography, typography and UI clearly belong to the same artist universe.
- The page feels authored rather than assembled from neutral components.
- Band/member imagery remains prominent.

### What we do NOT want to copy

- The exact red palette.
- The highly symmetrical layout.
- The conventional content structure beneath the art direction.
- Heavy use of boxed sections and borders.
- The specific industrial-metal visual clichés.

### Useful for

- Overall identity consistency.
- Colour-system thinking.
- Image treatment.
- Member / band presentation.

### Extracted rule

> Every section should look like it belongs to the same artistic era, even when its layout changes completely.

## REF-06 — Vinylist Release / Vinyl Object

**File:** `references/REF-06-vinylist-release-object.jpg`

### What we like

- The music itself is represented by a large physical object.
- The vinyl breaks the boundaries between layout zones.
- Strong black / light split creates composition without needing cards.
- Release metadata remains small while the musical object dominates.
- The design gives the release a sense of importance and tactility.

### What we do NOT want to copy

- Application-style UI.
- Playlist sidebar.
- Product-dashboard controls.
- Beige palette.
- Literal vinyl imagery unless relevant to an actual UTF release.

### Useful for

- Latest Release section.
- Music page.
- Album artwork presentation.

### Extracted rule

> Present releases as major visual objects, not as thumbnails attached to metadata.

## REF-07 — Dil-Luminati Live / Red Campaign

**File:** `references/REF-07-dil-luminati-energy.jpg`

### What we like

- Strong concert energy from full-width photography.
- Promotional typography can physically cross the page.
- Repeated text bands create campaign energy without requiring conventional UI decoration.
- Strong visual continuity between tour promotion, imagery and signup.
- A single accent colour creates immediate recognition.

### What we do NOT want to copy

- The exact red / black campaign.
- The card-based row of tour dates.
- Excessive promotional banners.
- The ecommerce / campaign landing-page feeling.

### Useful for

- Single-show state.
- Major concert announcement.
- Hero variants.
- Mailing-list treatment.
- Promotional transitions.

### Extracted rule

> Promotional elements can cut through the composition like physical print material instead of appearing as floating UI components.

# Combined Lessons

These references point toward a shared visual direction:

- photography first;
- large type used as composition;
- fewer visible containers;
- variable section rhythm;
- editorial rather than SaaS structure;
- strong contrast between huge and tiny elements;
- one controlled accent colour;
- print / poster influences;
- real band assets over decorative UI;
- practical information remains easy to scan.

They do **not** justify creating a generic Awwwards-style brutalist website.

UTF must still be recognisable through its own assets and visual world:

- Until They Fall photography;
- Gerard;
- the four-eye mark;
- Tower Seven;
- Sent To Die artwork;
- the band's actual live imagery.

# Reference Priority

When references conflict, prioritise them in this order:

1. UTF's real assets and identity.
2. `decision.md` and `design-rules.md`.
3. `visual-language.md`.
4. These references.

The references are inspiration, not requirements.
